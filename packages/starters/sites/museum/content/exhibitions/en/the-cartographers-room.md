A map is an argument about what matters, and the ninety hung here disagree loudly. The earliest, a portolan chart of 1611, gives the river three mouths; the survey of 1898 gives it one and moves it four hundred metres east. Between them lie two centuries of dredging, silt, and municipal wishful thinking.

The room is arranged as a cabinet rather than a corridor: maps at eye height on all four walls, low benches in the middle, and a single case holding the instruments — a plane table, two chains, a theodolite with a cracked bubble that its owner never replaced.

Visitors are welcome to sit as long as they like. The tracing paper and pencils on the benches are for you; what you draw is yours to take home.
