A good hammer disappears. You do not think about the hammer; you think about the nail. The tool becomes an extension of the hand, and all your attention flows through it to the work. This is the highest thing a tool can do — to vanish into the task.

Most software cannot resist being noticed. It wants your attention, because attention is what it sells. So it interrupts, it badges, it pulses a red dot in the corner of your eye. The tool has become a rival for the very focus it was supposed to serve.

## The economics of the red dot

There is a reason for this, and it is not incompetence. A tool that disappears cannot show you an ad. A tool that disappears does not generate engagement metrics. The incentives of much modern software point directly away from the thing that would make it good.

> The measure of a tool is not what it can do, but what it lets you forget about.

I try to notice which of my tools respect my attention and which of them farm it. The plain text editor that opens instantly and then gets out of the way. The notebook that has never once sent me a notification. These are the tools I return to for years, precisely because they ask for nothing.

## Choosing quiet tools

You can choose differently. You can prefer the tool that does less and interrupts never over the one that does everything and pings constantly. You can turn off the badges, mute the pulses, and let your software return to being an extension of your hand instead of a claim on your eyes.

It is a small rebellion, but a real one. Every tool you teach to disappear is a little more of your attention returned to the work — where it belonged all along.
