You can read a city if you slow down enough. Not the map of it — the map is a summary, a table of contents — but the actual surface: the patched asphalt, the ghost sign fading on a brick wall, the doorway bricked over decades ago and still faintly outlined.

Every building is a sentence someone wrote and someone else edited. The corner shop was a bank; you can tell from the disproportionate weight of the stone around the door. The wide sidewalk was once a streetcar line. The city is a palimpsest, written over and over, and the earlier drafts show through.

## Reading the corrections

What I love most are the corrections — the places where the city changed its mind. A grand staircase leading to a wall. A road that bends for no reason a driver can see, because it is still following the shape of a creek that was buried a century ago.

> Cities, like dreams, are made of desires and fears, wrote Calvino. So is every design, if you look at it long enough.

These are not mistakes. They are the record of a place negotiating with its own history, keeping what still worked and building around what did not. It is exactly how good design accretes: not by grand replacement, but by patient correction.

## The lesson for makers

If you make things — interfaces, products, essays — the city has a lesson for you. Nothing is written once. Everything you build will be edited by people you will never meet, adapted to uses you did not intend, patched and re-patched until the original draft is only faintly visible underneath.

Design as if that is true, because it is. Leave room for the corrections. Build things that can be edited kindly. The best work, like the best streets, is the kind that ages into something richer than what you first drew.
