The problem was not that I had run out of ideas. The problem was that I had too many, and they were all shouting at once, and I could not hear any single one of them clearly. So I put on boots and went out the door with nothing in my pockets but a key.

Walking is the oldest thinking technology we have. There is something about the pace of it — three miles an hour, roughly the speed of a thought — that lets the mind settle. Faster and you are just moving. Slower and you fidget. But at a walk, the body handles itself and the mind is finally free to wander somewhere useful.

## The pace of a thought

For the first mile the noise followed me. The unfinished emails, the half-decisions, the small anxieties that fill the gaps in a day. But somewhere in the second mile it began to thin out, and by the third there was only the road, the weather, and one clear question I had been avoiding for weeks.

> Solvitur ambulando — it is solved by walking. The Romans knew. So did every philosopher who ever wore out a pair of shoes.

I did not solve it, exactly. But I saw its actual shape for the first time, which is most of what solving is. The answer had been buried under all the noise, and it took two hours of moving through the world to dig it out.

## What the walk returns

I came home tired in the good way, the way that clears rather than depletes. Nothing on my to-do list had changed. But the thing I could not think about at my desk had become thinkable somewhere around the fourth mile, and that was worth more than any hour I have ever spent staring at a screen, waiting for clarity to arrive on its own.

It never does. You have to go out and walk it down.
