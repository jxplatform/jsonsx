Speed is the default virtue of software. Faster load times, fewer taps, less friction — the whole discipline of interaction design has quietly agreed that the goal is to get the user out of the way of the machine as quickly as possible.

But there is a category of experience that speed actively ruins. Reading a long essay. Looking at a photograph. Composing a difficult message to someone you love. For these, friction is not a bug. It is the point.

## The tyranny of the frictionless

When we remove every pause, we also remove every moment of consideration. The infinite scroll is frictionless by design, and it is frictionless precisely so that you never stop to ask whether you want to keep going. Frictionlessness is not neutral. It has a direction, and the direction is usually _more_.

Good design for slowness does the opposite. It builds in the pause. A generous margin is a pause. A single column of text with nothing competing for attention is a pause. Whitespace is not empty; it is the room a reader needs to think.

## What slowness asks of us

Designing this way is harder, because you cannot measure it with the usual dashboards. Time-on-page goes up, which your analytics may read as failure. Bounce rate is a meaningless number for a page whose entire purpose is to be read slowly and then closed.

The work, then, is to decide what an interface is _for_ before deciding how fast it should be. A checkout flow should be quick. An essay should be quiet. Knowing which is which — and having the nerve to design accordingly — is most of the job.
